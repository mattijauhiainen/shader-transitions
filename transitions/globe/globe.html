How the rectangle becomes a globe

The globe transition takes the same flat halftone grid every other transition uses and bends it onto a sphere. It does this in the vertex shader, per dot, with no mesh tessellation — each grid cell already has a flat position and a sphere position, and a single uniform uCurl ∈ [0, 1] linearly interpolates between them.

The sphere itself does not rotate — the dots are pinned to fixed spots on its surface. All the motion you see comes from the camera swinging around the globe on a tilted orbit, which is documented separately in globe-camera.html. This page is only about the rectangle → sphere mapping.

1. What "equirectangular" means

Equirectangular is the simplest possible way to wrap a rectangular image around a sphere: treat the image's x axis as longitude and its y axis as latitude, with both axes spaced linearly. No projection math, no area correction, no clever distortion — just a direct, proportional rename of the two axes.

A point on a sphere of radius R is fully described by two angles:

  • longitude λ ∈ [−π, π] — the angle around the vertical Y axis. λ = 0 points at +Z (the camera), λ = ±π points at −Z (the back).
  • latitude φ ∈ [−π/2, π/2] — the angle above or below the equator. φ = 0 is the equator, +π/2 is the north pole, −π/2 is the south pole.

The standard spherical-to-Cartesian formulas turn those two angles into a 3D position:

x = R · cos(φ) · sin(λ) y = R · sin(φ) z = R · cos(φ) · cos(λ)

Notice the cos(φ) in front of both x and z: as you approach a pole, cos(φ) → 0 and the horizontal circle of the sphere shrinks to a point. This is geometrically correct — every meridian really does meet at the pole — but it's also the source of every wart equirectangular has.

2. From pixel grid to (λ, φ)

The halftone grid has cols × rows cells. Equirectangular assigns each cell a longitude and latitude by linearly mapping its integer coordinates onto the angular ranges. The +0.5 picks the cell's center rather than its corner:

u = (col + 0.5) / cols ∈ (0, 1) v = (row + 0.5) / rows ∈ (0, 1) λ = (u − 0.5) · 2π ∈ (−π, π) φ = (v − 0.5) · π ∈ (−π/2, π/2)

The − 0.5 shifts the center of the image to angle 0. Without it, col = 0 would map to λ = 0 (the front of the globe) and so would col = cols − 1 (one cell shy of a full turn) — both edges of the flat image would land on the front, and during curl they would cross through each other to reach the back. With the −0.5 shift in place, the middle column sits at the front and the two edges curl symmetrically backwards to meet at the seam.

3. The two structural problems, and how this transition handles them

Equirectangular is famous for two distortions, both unavoidable consequences of cramming a sphere onto a rectangle:

(a) Horizontal stretch near the poles. The pixel row at v = 0.5 (the equator) covers a real-world arc of 2πR · cos(0) / cols = 2πR / cols. The pixel row at v = 0.95 (near a pole) covers 2πR · cos(0.45π) / cols ≈ 0.156 · 2πR / cols — six times less ground per dot. That's why a world map drawn equirectangularly makes Greenland huge and Antarctica a band stretching the full width of the page.

In this transition, the same stretch shows up as dot crowding: rows near the top and bottom of the image get squeezed into shrinking latitude circles, so their dots pile up into denser bands. The shader chooses not to fight this — no dot is culled or faded; every dot simply rides from its flat position to its sphere position. The pile-up reads cleaner than any cull-fade attempt.

(b) Pole singularity. At φ = ±π/2 exactly, cos(φ) = 0, so every cell in that row would map to the same point — the pole — and all cols dots would collapse onto each other. The mapping isn't a bijection at the poles.

This transition maps to the full latitude range and just accepts the pinch. The saving grace is the +0.5 cell-center offset: the top row sits at v = (rows − 0.5)/rows, not at v = 1. For a 22-row grid that's φ ≈ 85.9°, not 90° — so cos(φ) ≈ 0.07 rather than 0. The dots crowd into a very tight pole cap but never fully collapse onto a single point. It looks like a snug knot at each pole rather than a hard glitch.

4. Why this mapping, and not something fancier?

There are better sphere-onto-rectangle mappings if you care about equal area (Mollweide), equal angle (Mercator), or minimal worst-case distortion (Winkel tripel). They all involve either non-linear inverses or per-cell area weights. Equirectangular wins here for two reasons:

  • Trivial, exact addressing. Because λ and φ are proportional to col and row, the dot at column c, row r samples its own texel directly with (col + 0.5) / cols and (row + 0.5) / rows — no resampling, no inverse projection.
  • The grid stays the grid. The flat resting position is already a rectangular array; equirectangular treats it as a rectangular array. The mix(flatPos, spherePos, uCurl) blend has no gymnastics — both endpoints are addressed by the same (col, row).

5. Each cell has two homes

In transitions/globe/globe.vert.glsl the shader computes both positions for the dot it's drawing, then blends:

vec3 worldCenter = mix(flatPos, spherePos, uCurl);

The flat position is just the cell's grid coordinate centered on the origin — identical to where the resting halftone draws it. The sphere position comes from the equirectangular mapping above: column index becomes a longitude, row index becomes a latitude.

lon = ((col + 0.5) / cols − 0.5) · 2π lat = ((row + 0.5) / rows − 0.5) · π spherePos = uRADIUS · ( cos(lat)·sin(lon), sin(lat), cos(lat)·cos(lon) )

The −0.5 shift centers longitude on the +Z axis (the direction the camera looks at rest). So the middle column of the flat image lands on the front of the globe, and the left and right edges curl backwards to meet at the back — a poster rolling into a tube.

There is no spin term in the longitude: the dots stay put on the sphere. Whatever rotation you perceive comes from the camera orbit, not from moving these positions.

The curl bookends sit at uCurl = 0 exactly, which makes the handoff into and out of the neighbouring transitions a pixel-perfect match with the resting halftone — the dots are literally on their grid cells at those instants.

8. Why a billboard quad, not a mesh

Each dot is drawn as a two-triangle quad facing the camera, not as a piece of a tessellated sphere. The vertex shader computes the dot center on the sphere, then offsets the four corners along the camera's right/up basis vectors. The fragment shader treats the quad coords as (x, y) on a unit sphere, reconstructs z, and uses that to fake per-pixel sphere shading. Sphere impostor in two triangles, no mesh required.
