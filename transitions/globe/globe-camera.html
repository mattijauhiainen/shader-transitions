The flyover camera

While the dot grid is curling into a sphere, the camera doesn't sit still — it swings around onto a tilted great-circle orbit, drops to low altitude, pitches forward like a satellite skimming the surface, and rides one full lap around the globe. By the time the sphere starts unrolling back into a flat sheet, the camera has glided back to its rest pose.

The sphere itself is stationary — the dots are fixed on its surface. Every bit of apparent motion is the camera moving, not the globe spinning. That's a deliberate choice: a moving camera around a fixed globe gives parallax (genuine 3D depth), whereas spinning the surface texture just slides dots around.

0. The vocabulary

This page leans on a handful of 3D-camera and geometry terms. If any are unfamiliar, here they are in plain language — every one of them appears in the formulas below.

Camera pose: eye / target / up
A camera is fully described by three things, which is exactly what the code computes and hands to a lookAt function. eye = where the camera physically sits (a point). target = a point it is aimed at; the camera looks straight down the line from eye to target. up = which way is "up" for the camera, so it doesn't roll sideways (two cameras at the same spot aimed at the same target can still differ by a head-tilt; up pins that down).
Unit vector
An arrow of length exactly 1. It carries a direction only, no size. Several quantities below (upRadial, fwdTangent) are unit vectors.
Radial
The straight-outward direction from the sphere's center, like a spoke of a bicycle wheel pointing from the hub to the rim. The camera sits on such a spoke; upRadial is that spoke's direction.
Tangent
The direction of travel along a circle, perpendicular to the radial spoke. Picture driving around a roundabout: the radial points from the centre out to your car; the tangent points where your bonnet is aimed, along the curve.
Azimuth (az)
The angle telling you how far around the orbit circle the camera has swung — like the position of a clock hand. az = 0 and az = 2π (a full turn) are the same spot. Here the camera does one full turn.
Tilt
How much the orbit ring is tipped relative to the equator. Tilt 0 ⇒ the camera circles around the equator, staying level with the centre. Tilt 90° ⇒ it circles pole-to-pole, straight over the top and under the bottom. This transition ramps tilt up to 60°.
Pitch (down)
Nodding the camera's gaze downward from the horizon toward the surface below it, like dipping your chin. More pitch ⇒ more looking straight down at the planet.
Great circle
The biggest circle you can draw on a sphere; its flat plane slices through the sphere's centre (Earth's equator is one). The camera's orbit is a great circle.
Latitude / longitude
Angles locating a point on a sphere. Latitude is the north–south angle (0 at the equator, ±90° at the poles); longitude is the east–west angle around the vertical axis.
Lerp (linear interpolation) / blend
Mixing two values by a fraction c: a·(1−c) + b·c. At c = 0 you get a, at c = 1 you get b, smoothly in between. The whole camera is a lerp on curl between a rest pose and a flyover pose.

1. Two poses, blended by curl

The camera has exactly two states, and everything in between is a lerp on uCurl:

Because every flyover quantity is scaled by curl and the corresponding rest quantity by 1 − curl, both endpoints of the timeline (t = 0 and t = 1) collapse to the exact rest pose — the same one the resting halftone and every other transition use. That's what keeps the handoff pixel-aligned.

2. Why the orbit is tilted

The globe is stationary, so the only way to reveal more than one strip of it is to move the camera across latitudes. A horizontal orbit — the camera circling in the XZ plane, level with the centre — would keep the camera at the equator's latitude for the entire lap. It would circle the globe but only ever frame the equatorial band; the poles would never tip into view.

Tilting the orbital plane fixes that. The camera now rises above the equator on one side of the orbit and dips below it on the other, sweeping across both hemispheres in a single turn. It also gives vertical parallax — the camera moves up and down as well as around — which reads as real three-dimensional depth rather than a flat carousel.

3. The parameterization of the orbit

The orbit is a great circle at radius dist whose azimuth-zero point is fixed at +Z — the same spot the rest-pose camera sits at. That property is what makes the bookends line up: when orbitT = 0 and orbitT = 1, the camera is at (0, 0, dist) regardless of tilt.

The closed form comes from rotating +Z by azimuth az around an axis n = (sin(tilt), cos(tilt), 0) — a "north pole" of the orbit, tilted into the +X side of the XY plane:

eye = ( dist · cos(tilt) · sin(az), -dist · sin(tilt) · sin(az), dist · cos(az) )

Sanity-check the corners. At az = 0: sin(az) = 0, cos(az) = 1, so eye = (0, 0, dist). ✓ At az = π/2 with tilt = 60°: eye = (0.5·dist, −0.866·dist, 0) — on the equator-plus-South side, as expected for a great circle tilted that way.

Why anchor az = 0 at +Z? The tempting alternative is to take a flat circle and tilt it around the X axis. But that puts its az = 0 point at (0, sin(tilt), cos(tilt)) · dist rather than at (0, 0, dist). The moment curl engaged, the camera would jump up by sin(tilt) · dist — a visible lurch into the orbit, not a smooth glide. Rotating +Z around a tilted-in-XY axis instead keeps +Z exactly on the orbit at az = 0, so the start and end of the lap coincide with the rest pose.

Azimuth is driven by the orbit progress and negated:

az = − orbitT · 2π

The sign just picks the direction of travel — negative sends the camera counter-clockwise as seen looking down the +Y axis. With a stationary globe there's nothing for it to cancel or reinforce, so this is purely a look-and-feel choice; flipping the sign reverses the sweep.

4. The flyover orientation: orbital tangent, pitched down

At full curl, the camera's "up" is radial outward (away from the globe's center) and its "forward" is the orbital tangent, then pitched down toward the surface by PITCH_DOWN_DEG = 38°. This is the satellite-flyover convention: the planet fills the lower part of the frame, the horizon sits high.

The three vectors at the camera's position:

Concretely:

upRadial = normalize(eye) fwdTangent = d(eye)/d(az) (then sign-flipped to match negated az) forward = fwdTangent · cos(38°) − upRadial · sin(38°)

The pitch rotation lives in the plane spanned by fwdTangent and −upRadial: the tangent says "where I'm going," the inward radial says "down toward the surface," and 38° between them is the look direction.

The altitude/pitch coupling

Pitch isn't an independent knob — it depends on altitude. The horizon angle (from straight-down to the silhouette) is arcsin(R / (R + h)), and pitch should sit a little above that so some horizon and some surface are both visible:

The chosen values (1.20× radius, 38°) sit deliberately below the table's recommendation for that altitude — a shallower pitch than "balanced" because keeping more horizon in the frame reads better against the dot grid than a top-down planet view.

5. The blend between rest and flyover

Three things blend with curl:

tilt = TILT_PEAK · curl dist = D + (DIST_FLYOVER − D) · curl up = lerp( +Y , upRadial , curl )

The look direction is the subtle case. The obvious thing is to lerp the target point — but that's broken. At curl = 0.99 the target sits near the origin (centerward); at curl = 1.00 it jumps to eye + forward, which is right next to the camera. lookAt's normalize(eye − target) then flips abruptly between two very different regimes, and the view spins through 90°+ in a single frame.

The fix is to lerp the unit look direction instead, then reconstruct the target:

centerward = −upRadial lookDir = lerp( centerward , forward , curl ) target = eye + lookDir

6. Timeline at a glance

The orbit progress that drives az spans a slightly wider window than curl — it starts a hair after the curl begins and ends a hair after the uncurl begins, so the camera is already gliding by the time the sphere finishes forming and is still moving as it starts to unroll.

7. Summary

  1. Two poses, lerped by curl. Rest is (0, 0, D), looking at origin, up +Y. Flyover is a tilted-orbit position, looking along orbital tangent (pitched 38° down), up radial. Everything in between is linear in curl, so the bookends collapse to the rest pose exactly.
  2. Tilted orbit, not horizontal. The globe is fixed, so a horizontal orbit would frame only the equator band; tilt sweeps the camera through both hemispheres in one lap and adds vertical parallax.
  3. az = 0 anchored at +Z. The orbit is built by rotating +Z around a tilted-in-XY axis, not by tilting a circle around X. This keeps the start/end of the orbit at the rest position; the X-tilt alternative would jump the camera vertically the moment curl engaged.
  4. Azimuth sign is just direction. With a stationary globe the negation only chooses which way the camera sweeps; there's no spin to cancel or reinforce.
  5. Lerp look direction, not look target. Lerping the target point makes the view direction snap 90°+ in the last frame of curl; lerping a unit direction vector and reconstructing the target gives a smooth angular sweep.
  6. Altitude couples to pitch. The chosen 1.20× radius / 38° pitch favors more horizon in frame than the "balanced" recommendation for that altitude.
