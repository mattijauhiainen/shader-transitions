Ball shading in anamorphosis

The anamorphosis transition draws each halftone dot as a flat square that always turns to face the camera — a billboard. Squares are cheap: two triangles, no geometry to build, no normals to store. But during the flight the camera dives straight through the field of dots, and dots go rushing past the lens from a hand's width away. At that size a flat disc of solid colour reads as a paper cut-out. What the shot wants is something with volume.

So the fragment shader lies. It keeps drawing a flat square, and shades it as if a ball were sitting inside it. Thirteen lines of arithmetic, no extra geometry, no extra draw calls. This is how those lines work, and why each of them is shaped the way it is.

The square that pretends to be a ball

The vertex shader hands the fragment shader a coordinate called vCorner: where this fragment sits inside the billboard, running from (-1, -1) at one corner to (1, 1) at the other. The centre of the square is (0, 0). Read those two numbers as an (x, y) position on a ball of radius 1, and the third coordinate follows from the fact that a point on a unit sphere is one unit from its centre:

float r = length(vCorner);
float z = sqrt(max(0.0, 1.0 - r * r));

That is the whole geometric content of the trick. At the middle of the square r = 0 and z = 1: the top of the ball, pointing straight at us. At the circle inscribed in the square r = 1 and z = 0: the silhouette, where the surface has curved away until it is edge-on. In between, z traces the front hemisphere.

Which way is that surface facing?

To light the ball we need a normal: the direction the surface points at this pixel. On a unit sphere centred at the origin the normal is just the point itself — but the point is currently expressed in the billboard's own coordinates, and the light lives in world space. So we spell the same vector out in the camera's three axes, which are exactly the axes the billboard was built along:

vec3 N = normalize(vCorner.x * uCamRight + vCorner.y * uCamUp + z * uCamFwd);

uCamRight, uCamUp and uCamFwd are the camera's own right, up and back-toward-the-viewer directions, written in world space — anamorphosis.ts reads them out of the rows of the view matrix each frame. The vertex shader used the first two to swing the square around to face the camera. The fragment shader reuses all three to swing the ball around to face it too.

Two details in that one line are worth pausing on.

The sign of uCamFwd. Despite the name it points from the scene back toward the camera, because that is the convention the view matrix stores (the camera's local +z is behind it, looking down -z). That is what we want here: the z we computed is the height of the front hemisphere, the half facing us, so it has to be laid off in the direction of the viewer. Flip that sign and every ball would be lit as if seen from behind.

The normalize. On the unit disc the assembled vector already has length 1, so the call looks redundant. It is not, because the billboard is deliberately a little larger than the ball: the vertex shader inflates the quad by half a pixel of headroom so the anti-aliased rim has somewhere to fade out, and passes on vCorner = aPosition * scale. In that outer sliver r > 1, the max(0.0, …) clamps z to zero, and the vector is a sideways one of length r. Normalising keeps it a legal direction. Those fragments are all inside the fading edge anyway, and most of them are discarded a line earlier.

A lamp bolted to the lens

float diffuse = max(0.0, dot(N, vToCam));
float lit = 0.3 + 0.7 * diffuse;

vToCam is the unit direction from this dot's centre to the camera, computed once per dot in the vertex shader. Using it as the light direction puts the lamp exactly where the viewer is — a headlight. The dot product then measures how squarely each patch of surface faces us: 1 at the middle of the ball, sliding to 0 at the silhouette, which is Lambert's cosine law with the angle already baked into the vectors.

A headlight is a specific artistic choice, and it is the right one here for two reasons. It never puts a dot in shadow — every ball on screen keeps its own colour and stays legible as a halftone sample of the photograph, which the whole picture depends on. And it needs no light position to animate, so nothing about the shading has to be kept in sync with the flight path.

The 0.3 + is an ambient floor. Without it the silhouette goes to pure black, and against the black background that reads as the dot simply being smaller rather than round — the rim disappears instead of turning away. Three tenths is enough to keep the edge present while leaving most of the range for the falloff.

A free perspective cue. The normal is built in the camera basis, which is the same for every dot on screen, but it is lit against vToCam, which is per dot. For a dot dead ahead the two agree and the highlight sits in the middle. For a dot out at the edge of a wide frame the direction home is tilted by up to half the field of view, and the highlight slides toward the centre of the screen — exactly as it would on real balls lit from the viewer's position. It costs nothing; it falls out of using the honest direction rather than reusing uCamFwd for both jobs.

Turning the whole thing off at the bookends

Anamorphosis begins and ends parked at a standstill, at the exact spot where the field of dots collapses back into the ordinary flat halftone. The slides on either side of the transition draw that halftone with flat discs. If the transition's first and last frames were shaded balls, the handover would pop.

float intensity = mix(1.0, lit, uSphereShading);

At uSphereShading = 0 the intensity is a flat 1 and the dot is a solid disc of its own colour, pixel-identical to what the neighbouring transitions draw. At 1 it is the full ball. anamorphosis.ts ramps it up over the first 5% of the transition and back down over the last 5%:

if (t < 0.05) sphereShading = t / 0.05;
else if (t > 0.95) sphereShading = (1 - t) / 0.05;
else sphereShading = 1;

By the time the shading is fully on, the camera has already started moving and the dots have started to separate in depth, so the ramp hides inside motion that is drawing the eye anyway. This is the same discipline the rest of the transition follows: the flight's easing parks the camera at both ends, and the anti-aliasing is computed in screen pixels so the rims match too. Every property that differs from the resting halftone has to be brought back to it before the last frame.

The last line

fragColor = vec4(vColor.rgb * intensity * alpha, alpha);

Three things are multiplied together here. intensity is the shading we just built. alpha is coverage: the anti-aliased rim, computed a few lines earlier in screen pixels so that a dot's edge is feathered over exactly one pixel however big it is on screen, times vFade, the opacity of the image this dot belongs to (image B fades up once the camera is through image A).

Multiplying the colour by its own alpha makes the output premultiplied, which is why anamorphosis.ts pairs the shader with:

gl.blendFunc(gl.ONE, gl.ONE_MINUS_SRC_ALPHA);

The usual SRC_ALPHA, ONE_MINUS_SRC_ALPHA would have the blender apply the coverage, but the shader is already scaling the colour by two factors and the fade is a third; doing the multiply here keeps all of them in one place and keeps overlapping rims composing correctly rather than each re-weighting a colour that was already weighted.

What this deliberately does not do

  • It does not move the fragment's depth. The impostor is shaded like a ball but occupies the depth of a flat billboard, so where two dots interpenetrate they still sort as discs. Writing gl_FragDepth would fix that and cost the early-z rejection of every dot on screen — a bad trade for dots that are a few pixels across and rarely intersect.
  • There is no specular highlight. A headlight specular would sit dead centre on every ball at once and read as a field of identical white pips, flattening the very thing the shading is for.
  • The balls do not shade each other. No shadows, no ambient occlusion. At a hundred thousand dots per frame the point of the impostor is that it is nearly free.

Summary

  1. Read the billboard coordinate vCorner as a point on a unit ball and recover the missing height with z = sqrt(1 - r²). That is the impostor.
  2. Spell that point out in the camera's own axes to get a world-space normal — no per-dot data, three uniforms.
  3. Light it from the camera's position. Cosine falloff over an ambient floor of 0.3, so rims turn away instead of vanishing.
  4. mix(1.0, lit, uSphereShading) lets the whole effect be faded out at both ends, so the transition hands back the same flat halftone it was given.
  5. Multiply by coverage on the way out, because the draw is set up for premultiplied alpha.
