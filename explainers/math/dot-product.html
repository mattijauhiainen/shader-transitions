The Dot Product

The dot product takes two vectors and returns a single number. Despite being just a number, that number captures something deeply geometric: how much one vector points in the direction of another.

The two definitions

There are two formulas for the dot product. They look totally different. They give exactly the same answer. This is not a coincidence — it's the entire reason the dot product is useful.

Algebraic: multiply matching components, add them up.

a · b = a.x * b.x + a.y * b.y + a.z * b.z

Geometric: the product of the two vectors' lengths, times the cosine of the angle between them.

a · b = |a| |b| cos(θ)

The first form is what you compute. The second form is what it means.

Why both formulas are the same

If you take the unit basis vectors x̂ = (1, 0, 0), ŷ = (0, 1, 0), ẑ = (0, 0, 1), you can verify that x̂ · x̂ = 1 (same direction, angle 0, cos(0) = 1) and x̂ · ŷ = 0 (perpendicular, angle 90°, cos(90°) = 0). Both definitions agree on these cases. From there, the bilinearity of the operation propagates the agreement to all other vectors. We won't derive the equivalence here — just trust that it holds, and use whichever form is convenient.

What the sign tells you

Because |a| and |b| are always positive (they're lengths), the sign of a · b is determined entirely by cos(θ):

This is the dot product's superpower: a single arithmetic check tells you whether two vectors face the same way, opposite ways, or are perpendicular.

The projection picture

The geometric formula |a| |b| cos(θ) describes the length of b's shadow when light shines perpendicular to a, multiplied by the length of a. If a is a unit vector (length 1), then a · b is exactly the length of b's shadow onto the line of a.

This is why "projecting onto an axis" in graphics code is just a dot product. If x̂ is the world's x-axis (a unit vector), then point · x̂ is the point's x-coordinate. If uCamForward is the camera's forward direction (also a unit vector), then (point - uCamPos) · uCamForward is the point's depth in front of the camera.

Where this lives in the halftones project

Dot products are everywhere in your codebase. A few examples:

Luma — converting RGB to a single brightness

float lumaValue = dot(color.rgb, uLUMA);

Here uLUMA is a constant vector of weights, conventionally something like (0.299, 0.587, 0.114). The dot product collapses three color channels into one number by weighted sum. This is the same operation as the algebraic definition above — no angle involved, just "multiply matching components and add."

Lambertian lighting — how brightly a surface is lit

float diffuse = max(dot(normal, lightDir), 0.0);

Both normal and lightDir are unit vectors. The dot product is cos(θ) where θ is the angle between the surface and the light. When the light hits the surface head-on, cos(0) = 1 — full brightness. As the angle widens, brightness drops to zero. Past 90°, the surface faces away from the light and we clamp to 0 with max.

Depth in front of a camera (orbit transition)

float depth = dot(toPoint, uCamForward);

toPoint is the vector from camera to a world point. uCamForward is the camera's unit forward axis. The dot product is the length of toPoint's shadow onto uCamForward — exactly the depth. A positive result means in front of the camera, negative means behind.

One trick worth remembering

If a and b are both unit vectors, then a · b = cos(θ) directly, with no length factors to worry about. This means the dot product of two unit vectors is the cosine of the angle between them. You'll see this used constantly to avoid ever computing an explicit angle — comparing dot products is much cheaper than calling acos.

Want to test if vector v is within 30° of direction d? Don't compute the angle — just check dot(v, d) > cos(30°), where the right-hand side is a precomputed constant. Same answer, no trigonometry at runtime.
