Rotation Matrices

A rotation matrix is just a specific kind of linear transformation: one that rotates vectors around a fixed origin without stretching, shearing, or reflecting them. As with all matrices, the entire matrix is determined by where the basis vectors go. So the entire question "what does the rotation matrix look like?" reduces to: "where do x̂, ŷ, and ẑ land after rotating?"

This explainer starts in 2D, where the picture is clear, then lifts to 3D.

2D rotation: where do x̂ and ŷ go?

Imagine rotating the entire plane by angle θ counter-clockwise. The basis vectors swing too:

  • x̂ = (1, 0) was pointing right. After rotating by θ, it points in direction (cos θ, sin θ).
  • ŷ = (0, 1) was pointing up. After rotating by θ, it points in direction (-sin θ, cos θ).

Why? Each basis vector has length 1 and starts at a known angle (0° for x̂, 90° for ŷ). After rotating by θ, x̂ ends up at angle θ, which in coordinates is (cos θ, sin θ) by the definition of cosine and sine. ŷ ends up at angle 90° + θ, and using the identities cos(90° + θ) = -sin θ and sin(90° + θ) = cos θ, that's (-sin θ, cos θ).

From the previous explainer: columns of the matrix are the destinations of the basis vectors. So we just stack these two destinations as columns:

R(θ) = ┌ cos θ   -sin θ ┐
       └ sin θ    cos θ ┘
        ↑          ↑
        where      where
        x̂ went     ŷ went

That's it. No memorization. The matrix is built by asking "where do my basis vectors go," and trigonometry gives the answer.

From 2D to 3D: rotation needs an axis

In 2D, you only need a single angle to specify a rotation. In 3D, rotation needs more: you have to say which axis you're rotating around. Spinning a globe around a vertical pole is different from tipping it forward; both are rotations.

The simplest 3D rotations are around one of the three coordinate axes. Each of them is, in essence, a 2D rotation that happens in the plane perpendicular to the axis:

  • Rotation around x: the x-axis stays fixed; y and z swap places. (Like tipping a book forward.)
  • Rotation around y: the y-axis stays fixed; z and x swap places. (Like spinning on a vertical pole.)
  • Rotation around z: the z-axis stays fixed; x and y swap places. (Like the 2D case above, embedded in 3D.)

Each of these is the same trigonometry as the 2D case — just applied to whichever pair of coordinates are rotating. The third coordinate (the axis you're rotating around) is left alone.

The three matrices

Let's build them by asking the same question as before: where does each basis vector go?

Rotation around the x-axis: Rx(θ)

The x-axis is fixed — so x̂ goes to itself, (1, 0, 0). The y and z axes rotate by θ in the y-z plane. Treating that as a 2D rotation:

  • ŷ = (0, 1, 0) lands at (0, cos θ, sin θ)
  • ẑ = (0, 0, 1) lands at (0, -sin θ, cos θ)

Stack these as columns of a 3×3 matrix:

Rx(θ) = ┌ 1    0       0   ┐
        │ 0  cos θ  -sin θ │
        └ 0  sin θ   cos θ ┘

Rotation around the y-axis: Ry(θ)

The y-axis is fixed — so ŷ stays at (0, 1, 0). The other two rotate in the z-x plane:

  • ẑ = (0, 0, 1) lands at (sin θ, 0, cos θ)
  • x̂ = (1, 0, 0) lands at (cos θ, 0, -sin θ)
Ry(θ) = ┌ cos θ   0   sin θ ┐
        │   0     1    0    │
        └ -sin θ  0   cos θ ┘

Rotation around the z-axis: Rz(θ)

The z-axis is fixed — ẑ stays at (0, 0, 1). The 2D rotation happens in the x-y plane, exactly as in our earlier 2D derivation:

Rz(θ) = ┌ cos θ  -sin θ   0 ┐
        │ sin θ   cos θ   0 │
        └   0       0     1 ┘

Why the patterns differ

Look at the three matrices side by side. Each has a fixed "axis row" and a fixed "axis column" that contain 1 on the diagonal and 0 everywhere else. The 2×2 rotation block lives in the other two rows/columns.

What's tricky is Ry: the off-diagonal terms look "backwards" compared to Rx and Rz. The sin θ sits at row 0, col 2 (positive), and the -sin θ sits at row 2, col 0. In Rx and Rz the sign is the other way around.

This is not a mistake or a quirk — it falls out of the right-handed-orientation convention. The cyclic order of basis-vector cross products is x̂ × ŷ = ẑ, ŷ × ẑ = x̂, ẑ × x̂ = ŷ. For Rx, the rotating pair is (ŷ → ẑ), which follows the cyclic order. For Rz, it's (x̂ → ŷ), also cyclic. But for Ry, the rotating pair is (ẑ → x̂), also cyclic — and that's precisely what flips the sign positions. The middle axis's rotation runs against the visual layout of the matrix, even though it's geometrically consistent with the other two.

If you want to avoid memorizing three slightly different sign patterns, just rederive each one as you need it by asking "where does each basis vector go?" The trigonometry doesn't lie.

The right-hand rule sets the sign

"Counter-clockwise" is ambiguous in 3D — it depends which way you're looking. The convention is the right-hand rule: point your right thumb along the axis of rotation. Your fingers curl in the positive-θ direction.

So if you point your right thumb along +x, your fingers curl from +y toward +z. That's why Rx(90°) sends ŷ to ẑ: it's the positive direction of rotation around x.

Test it on the interactive: select X-axis rotation, set θ = 90°. The blue (ŷ) arrow should swing onto the green (ẑ) direction. If your code does the opposite, you've got a sign flipped somewhere in the matrix — the standard symptom is "my world appears mirrored" or "my rotation goes the wrong way."

Column-major storage in code

The matrices above are written in math notation (rows of the matrix, top to bottom, left to right). In your code, they're stored in a flat Float32Array in column-major order. The mapping for a 3×3 matrix is:

┌ a  b  c ┐                              storage:
│ d  e  f │   ↔   [a, d, g,  b, e, h,  c, f, i]
└ g  h  i ┘            ↑       ↑       ↑
                    column 0  col 1  col 2

For your Rx in column-major storage:

R_x[0] = 1    R_x[3] = 0       R_x[6] = 0
R_x[1] = 0    R_x[4] = cos θ   R_x[7] = -sin θ
R_x[2] = 0    R_x[5] = sin θ   R_x[8] = cos θ

The -sin θ in the math matrix lives at row 1, column 2 — which is storage index 2*3 + 1 = 7. The sin θ at row 2, column 1 is at 1*3 + 2 = 5. Same numbers, different layout in memory.

The 4×4 versions used in graphics are identical except padded with an extra row and column from the identity matrix (the homogeneous coordinate, for translations). For Rx in your mat4.ts:

const Rx = new Float32Array([
  1, 0, 0, 0,         // column 0
  0, c, s, 0,         // column 1  ← c at row 1, s at row 2
  0, -s, c, 0,        // column 2  ← -s at row 1, c at row 2
  0, 0, 0, 1,         // column 3
]);

The pattern from before holds: read across — that gives you columns, not rows. If you try to fill the matrix by reading across a row of the math notation and writing four consecutive array entries, you end up with the transpose. (That's the bug we hit when first implementing this.)

Composing rotations

To rotate first around x and then around y, you multiply the matrices in that order — right to left:

combined = R_y · R_x

When applied to a vector v: combined · v = R_y · (R_x · v). Rx acts first.

Three things to remember about composing rotations:

  1. Order matters. Ry·Rx ≠ Rx·Ry in general. Try it: tip a book forward 90° then yaw 90° versus yaw first then tip — you end up facing different directions.
  2. The result is still a rotation. The product of two rotation matrices is another rotation matrix. The space of rotations is closed under composition (in math language, it's a group — the special orthogonal group SO(3)).
  3. Gimbal lock. Chaining three axis rotations (Euler angles: rotate around x, then y, then z) can hit a degenerate configuration where two of the rotation axes line up, leaving you with only two degrees of freedom instead of three. This is one reason quaternions are popular for 3D rotation — they don't have this problem. But for graphics where you compose a few rotations per frame and never chain them long-term, Euler angles are fine.

Connection to your mat4.ts

Your rotateX(out, m, angle) function follows the standard convention: it computes out = m · R_x(angle). This is multiplication on the right, which means the rotation is applied in the local coordinate system of whatever transformation m already represents.

If you want to rotate in world space instead — applying the rotation before any other transformations m represents — you'd compute out = R_x(angle) · m instead. Both are useful in different contexts; the convention in most graphics libraries is the "right-multiply for local rotation" version, which is what you have.

For typical use cases — "start with identity, then rotate, then translate, then scale" — you build the model matrix up by chained right-multiplications, and the resulting model matrix applies its transformations in the same order to a point in object space.
