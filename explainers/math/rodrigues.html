Rodrigues' Rotation Formula

The rotation-matrix explainer built rotations around the three coordinate axes — x, y, and z. But often the axis you want to spin around is none of those: it's some arbitrary unit vector pointing off at an angle. Rodrigues' formula rotates a vector around any axis directly, without first changing coordinate systems.

It looks cryptic when you first meet it:

v_rot = v cos θ  +  (k × v) sin θ  +  k (k · v)(1 - cos θ)

...but every piece falls out of one idea: split the vector into the part that moves and the part that doesn't, then rotate only the part that moves. This explainer derives it from that idea, leaning on the dot product and cross product you've already seen.

The setup

We're given:

  • a unit axis k (length 1) to rotate around,
  • a vector v to rotate,
  • an angle θ, positive by the right-hand rule (right thumb along k, fingers curl the positive direction).

The trick that makes everything tractable: a rotation around k leaves anything along k untouched, and spins anything perpendicular to k in a circle. So if we can break v into "along k" and "perpendicular to k" pieces, we know exactly what happens to each.

Step 1 — Split v into parallel and perpendicular parts

The component of v lying along k is its projection onto k. From the dot-product explainer, the projection of v onto a unit vector k is (k · v) k:

v∥ = (k · v) k          ← the part along the axis
v⊥ = v − v∥           ← whatever's left, perpendicular to k

By construction v = v∥ + v⊥, and v⊥ is perpendicular to k (subtracting the projection removes exactly the parallel part). The rotation will:

  • leave v∥ exactly as it is — it's on the axis, it doesn't move;
  • spin v⊥ by θ within the plane perpendicular to k.

Step 2 — Build a 2D coordinate frame in the rotation plane

Rotating v⊥ is now a flat, 2D problem — but 2D rotation (from the rotation explainer) needs two perpendicular axes to express the answer as cos θ · (first axis) + sin θ · (second axis). We already have one: v⊥ itself. We need a second axis, perpendicular to v⊥, in the same plane, and the same length.

The cross product hands it to us. Recall k × v is perpendicular to both k and v. Define:

w = k × v

Two facts make w the perfect second axis:

  • Same length as v⊥. Since |k × v| = |k| |v| sin α = |v| sin α (with |k| = 1, α the angle between them), and |v⊥| = |v| sin α too — they match. Also k × v = k × v⊥, because the parallel part contributes nothing to the cross product.
  • Perpendicular to v⊥, in the rotation plane. w is perpendicular to k, so it lies in the plane; and it's perpendicular to v, hence to v⊥.

So {v⊥, w} is a clean perpendicular pair of equal length spanning the rotation plane — a little 2D coordinate system, tilted in 3D.

Step 3 — Rotate within the plane, then add the axis part back

In that 2D frame, rotating v⊥ by θ is the textbook 2D rotation: stay cos θ along the starting axis, swing sin θ toward the perpendicular one.

v⊥_rot = v⊥ cos θ + w sin θ

The parallel part rides along unchanged, so the full rotated vector is:

v_rot = v∥ + v⊥_rot
      = v∥ + v⊥ cos θ + w sin θ

Now substitute v⊥ = v − v∥ and v∥ = (k · v) k and w = k × v, and collect:

v_rot = (k·v)k + (v − (k·v)k) cos θ + (k × v) sin θ
      = v cos θ + (k × v) sin θ + k (k · v)(1 − cos θ)

That's Rodrigues' formula. It's just "leave the axis part alone, rotate the rest in its plane" — written out in dot- and cross-product arithmetic. Each term has a job:

  • v cos θ — the original vector, shrinking as it tips away (the cos part of the in-plane swing, applied to all of v);
  • (k × v) sin θ — the sideways swing, perpendicular to both axis and vector;
  • k (k · v)(1 − cos θ) — a correction that restores the parallel component the cos θ term over-shrank, so the axis-aligned part stays put.

Sanity checks

Two angles let you verify the formula by eye:

  • θ = 0: cos 0 = 1, sin 0 = 0, 1 − cos 0 = 0, so v_rot = v. No rotation, no change. ✓
  • v parallel to k: then k × v = 0 and (k · v) k = v. The formula collapses to v cos θ + k(k·v)(1−cos θ) = v cos θ + v(1−cos θ) = v. A vector on the axis is unaffected, for any θ. ✓

The 180° case — and why it bit us

Set θ = 180°: cos θ = −1, sin θ = 0, 1 − cos θ = 2:

v_rot = −v + 0 + 2 k (k · v)
      = 2 v∥ − v

This is a reflection of v through the axis. If — and only if — v is perpendicular to k, then v∥ = 0 and this is exactly −v. That's the property the orbit transition relies on: to flip the camera's forward vector to its exact opposite, rotate by π around an axis perpendicular to forward.

The reason an axis is needed at all: v and −v are antipodal, and there's no unique way to rotate one into the other — you can sweep through any plane containing v. Picking the axis picks the plane. (Try it above: the path from v to −v sweeps through wherever w points; choose a different k and the half-circle tilts.)

Connection to the code

In transitions/orbit/orbit.ts the formula is implemented directly on Vec3 tuples — no matrix needed, since we're rotating a single vector, not a whole scene:

// Rodrigues' rotation: rotate v around unit axis k by angle theta.
function rotateAboutAxis(v: Vec3, k: Vec3, theta: number): Vec3 {
  const c = Math.cos(theta);
  const s = Math.sin(theta);
  const kv = cross(k, v);
  const kd = dot(k, v) * (1 - c);
  return [
    v[0] * c + kv[0] * s + k[0] * kd,
    v[1] * c + kv[1] * s + k[1] * kd,
    v[2] * c + kv[2] * s + k[2] * kd,
  ];
}

Line up the three contributions with the three terms of the formula: v * c is v cos θ, kv * s is (k × v) sin θ, and k * kd is k (k · v)(1 − cos θ). It's the math on this page, transcribed.

The orbit code uses it to ease the camera's look direction from +velocity to −velocity across the dot-plane crossing:

const movementForward = normalize(vel);

const turnStart = 0.55;
const turnEnd = 0.85;
const s = clamp((t - turnStart) / (turnEnd - turnStart), 0, 1);
const blend = s * s * (3 - 2 * s);                 // smoothstep 0 → 1

const upDotF = dot(worldUpHint, movementForward);
const rotationAxis = normalize([
  worldUpHint[0] - upDotF * movementForward[0],
  worldUpHint[1] - upDotF * movementForward[1],
  worldUpHint[2] - upDotF * movementForward[2],
]);
const cameraForward = rotateAboutAxis(movementForward, rotationAxis, -blend * Math.PI);

blend ramps 0 → 1 over the turn window, so the angle −blend·π sweeps 0 → −π — the camera looks straight ahead before the turn, fully reversed after. The negative sign just picks which way it swings (see "picking the axis" below).

How the axis is chosen

The whole flip hinges on getting rotationAxis right. We need an axis that is perpendicular to movementForward — that's the non-negotiable requirement, because only then does θ = π land exactly on −movementForward (the 180° case above). Any axis with a component along forward would leave v∥ ≠ 0, and the flip would miss the target direction.

Those three lines are Gram–Schmidt: take the up vector and subtract off its forward-parallel part, leaving only the perpendicular remainder.

rotationAxis = normalize( up − (up · fwd) · fwd )
                          └──────┬──────┘
                          the part of up that points along fwd,
                          removed — what's left is ⟂ fwd

This is exactly the v⊥ = v − (k·v)k projection from Step 1, used in reverse: there we removed the perpendicular part to isolate the axis component; here we remove the parallel part to isolate a perpendicular direction. Subtracting a vector's projection onto another always leaves something perpendicular to it.

Two choices remain free, and both are deliberate:

  • Which perpendicular direction? Infinitely many vectors are perpendicular to forward — they fill a whole plane. We seed from worldUpHint specifically so the camera flips through its own up/down plane (a pitch-like tumble) rather than yawing sideways. Seeding from a different vector would tilt the plane the flip sweeps through, the same way changing k tilts the half-circle in the demo above.
  • Which way around? A perpendicular axis still leaves two opposite spin directions (+axis vs −axis, equivalently +θ vs −θ). The code rotates by −blend·π rather than +blend·π to make the camera tumble the visually preferred way through the crossing. Flipping that sign reverses the tumble without changing the start or end direction.

One subtlety worth noting: this construction is only stable while worldUpHint isn't parallel to forward. If up ever lined up with the direction of travel, up − (up·fwd)fwd would collapse toward zero and normalize would have nothing to work with. On this flight path the up hint stays roughly vertical while forward is dominated by the z-crossing, so they're comfortably non-parallel through the turn window.

Why not a rotation matrix?

You could build a 3×3 rotation matrix for an arbitrary axis (it's Rodrigues' formula reorganised into matrix entries) and multiply. That's worth it when you rotate many vectors by the same rotation — build the matrix once, reuse it. Here we rotate one vector per frame by a different angle each time, so the direct vector form is leaner: three multiply-adds and one cross product, no matrix to assemble. It's the same arithmetic either way — the matrix just pre-packages it.
