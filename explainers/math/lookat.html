lookAt — Building the View Matrix

The view matrix is the transformation that takes points from world space into camera space: a coordinate system where the camera sits at the origin and looks down the −z axis. Everything in your scene gets multiplied by this matrix before projection, so the GPU can pretend the camera is at the origin and only do one kind of geometry.

The lookAt(eye, target, up) function builds this matrix from three intuitive inputs: where the camera is, what it's looking at, and which direction is "up." This explainer walks through why the resulting matrix has the structure it does.

The illusion: the camera never actually moves

In a real-world camera, the camera moves and the world stays still. In a rendering pipeline, it's the opposite: the camera is fixed at the origin, and the world moves around it.

This convention exists because the GPU only knows how to project from a fixed viewpoint. Building all the math around "camera at origin, looking down −z, with +y up" lets the projection matrix be a simple, single formula. To make the scene look like it's seen from somewhere else, we have to do the inverse: transform the world so that whatever was in front of the camera ends up in front of the origin.

That transform is the view matrix. The view matrix is the inverse of the camera's would-be position and orientation.

What lookAt needs to figure out

Given the three inputs:

  • eye — where the camera is, in world coordinates
  • target — what point the camera is looking at
  • up — the world's "up" direction (almost always (0, 1, 0))

...it computes a transform that does two things:

  1. Rotates the world so that the camera's forward direction aligns with the world's −z axis (and the camera's up aligns with +y, right aligns with +x).
  2. Translates the world so that the camera ends up sitting at the origin.

Both of these are "inverses" of where the camera would actually be. If the camera is 10 units to the right of the origin, the world has to slide 10 units to the left. If the camera is looking 30° to the right, the world has to rotate 30° to the left.

Step 1: Build the camera's basis vectors

The first thing lookAt does is figure out the camera's own coordinate frame — its forward, right, and up directions in world space.

1. f = normalize(target - eye)         // forward
2. s = normalize(cross(f, up))         // right (s for "side")
3. u = cross(s, f)                     // re-orthogonalized up

Forward

Subtracting eye from target gives a vector pointing from the camera toward what it's looking at. Normalizing makes it unit length so it's purely a direction. That's the camera's forward axis.

Right

The cross product of forward and the world-up hint produces a vector perpendicular to both — which is the camera's right axis by the right-hand rule. (See the cross product explainer if this isn't yet obvious.)

Normalizing is needed because the world-up and forward might not be perpendicular, so the cross product's magnitude could be anything; we just want the direction.

Re-orthogonalized up

The world-up hint we passed in might not be perfectly perpendicular to the camera's forward — for instance, if the camera is tilted to look at the ground, the world's +y axis isn't really "up" relative to where the camera is pointing. We need a true "up" that's perpendicular to both forward and right.

Easy: take the cross product of right and forward. That gives us a vector perpendicular to both, with the correct orientation for a right-handed coordinate frame. This vector replaces the user's approximate up hint.

At this point we have three unit vectors s, u, f that form an orthonormal basis describing the camera's orientation in the world.

Step 2: Build the rotation (transpose of the basis)

If you stack the three basis vectors s, u, f as columns of a matrix, you get a transformation that takes the world axes to the camera's basis vectors — i.e., it rotates the world axes into the camera's orientation. That's the opposite of what we want.

We want to rotate the world so the camera's orientation lines up with the world axes. That's the inverse transformation. For orthonormal matrices (which is what a pure rotation matrix is), the inverse is just the transpose. So we stack s, u, −f as rows:

┌  s.x   s.y   s.z  ┐
│  u.x   u.y   u.z  │
│ -f.x  -f.y  -f.z  │
└  0     0     0    ┘

Why −f and not f? Convention. The graphics convention is that a camera looking down its forward axis sees things at negative z in camera space. So the camera's forward direction (f) must map to the world's −z direction after this rotation. Negating f in the matrix takes care of that.

Step 3: Add the translation

We still need to slide the world so the camera ends up at the origin. The naïve answer is "translate by −eye." But because we just rotated the world, we need to express that translation in the rotated frame: each row of the rotation tells us where one of the new axes is pointing, so the translation along that axis is the dot product of the row with −eye.

That gives the last column:

┌  s.x   s.y   s.z  -dot(s, eye) ┐
│  u.x   u.y   u.z  -dot(u, eye) │
│ -f.x  -f.y  -f.z   dot(f, eye) │
└  0     0     0     1           ┘

The bottom 1 is the standard homogeneous-coordinate slot (see the matrix-multiplication explainer for why it has to be there).

This is the complete view matrix. Three independent steps, derived from clear geometric requirements.

While you're playing:

  • Move the eye in a circle around a target. Notice how the basis vectors swing but always stay perpendicular to each other.
  • Place the eye and target at the same position — the math breaks (forward is the zero vector and can't be normalized). lookAt isn't defined for that case. Most implementations guard against it.
  • If you make the camera look directly along the world-up axis (i.e. straight up or down), forward becomes parallel to up and the cross product collapses to zero. Another degenerate case. In practice you either avoid pointing straight up/down, or you change the up reference.

Why this matrix structure, geometrically

Take any world point p and multiply by the view matrix. The three dot products you get out are:

p_view.x =  dot(s, p - eye)     // how far right of the camera p is
p_view.y =  dot(u, p - eye)     // how far above the camera p is
p_view.z = -dot(f, p - eye)     // how far in front of the camera p is (negated)

(The matrix-vector multiplication algebra works out to exactly this, after you fold in the translation terms.)

Each row of the rotation part is a basis vector of the camera. Dot-producting world coordinates with that basis vector measures "how much this point projects onto that camera axis." This is the dot product's projection meaning in action (see explainer).

The result is camera-space coordinates: how far right, how far up, how far in front. Perfect input for the projection matrix, which then maps these into clip space.

Connection to your mat4.ts

Your implementation:

export function lookAt(out, eye, target, up) {
  const f = normalize([target[0] - eye[0], target[1] - eye[1], target[2] - eye[2]]);
  const s = normalize(cross(f, up));
  const u = cross(s, f);

  // column 0
  out[0] = s[0];  out[1] = u[0];  out[2] = -f[0];  out[3] = 0;
  // column 1
  out[4] = s[1];  out[5] = u[1];  out[6] = -f[1];  out[7] = 0;
  // column 2
  out[8] = s[2];  out[9] = u[2];  out[10] = -f[2]; out[11] = 0;
  // column 3 — translation
  out[12] = -dot(s, eye);  out[13] = -dot(u, eye);  out[14] = dot(f, eye);  out[15] = 1;
  return out;
}

Each group of 4 entries is a column of the matrix on paper. Column 0 has the x-components of s, u, and −f (the first column of the rotation, which is what gets dot-producted with the world point's x). Column 3 is the translation, computed as the camera offset projected onto each new basis.

Putting it together

The full pipeline for a vertex in your shader:

gl_Position = projection · view · model · vec4(localPos, 1.0)

Reading right to left:

  1. model places the vertex into world space.
  2. view (built by lookAt) transforms world space into camera space — the camera ends up at the origin.
  3. projection turns camera space into clip space — perspective foreshortening happens here.

Each matrix has one clear job. lookAt is the boundary between "where is everything in the world" and "where is everything relative to the camera." Once you've built it correctly, the rest of the pipeline doesn't know or care where the camera "really" is — as far as it's concerned, the camera is always at the origin looking down −z.
