The Cross Product

The dot product takes two vectors and returns a number. The cross product takes two vectors and returns another vector — one that's perpendicular to both inputs. This operation only really makes sense in 3D, and it's the workhorse for building coordinate systems, computing surface normals, and orienting cameras.

The formula

For 3D vectors a = (a.x, a.y, a.z) and b = (b.x, b.y, b.z):

a × b = ( a.y * b.z  -  a.z * b.y,
          a.z * b.x  -  a.x * b.z,
          a.x * b.y  -  a.y * b.x )

That looks intimidating. There's a pattern that makes it memorable:

  • For the x component of the result, use y and z of the inputs.
  • For the y component, use z and x.
  • For the z component, use x and y.

The indices cycle: x → y → z → x → y → z. For each output component, you "skip" the index of that component and use the other two, in the cyclic order. Each component is a[next] * b[next-next] - a[next-next] * b[next]. The mnemonic isn't elegant, but writing it out a few times burns it in.

What it produces

The cross product gives you a vector with three properties:

  1. Direction: perpendicular to both a and b.
  2. Orientation: determined by the right-hand rule (explained below).
  3. Magnitude: equal to |a| |b| sin(θ) — the area of the parallelogram formed by a and b.

The right-hand rule

Perpendicular to a plane has two possible directions — straight up or straight down. Which one does the cross product pick?

Use your right hand. Point your fingers in the direction of a. Curl them toward b. Your thumb now points in the direction of a × b.

This is why order matters: a × b = -(b × a). If you swap the inputs, your curled fingers go the other way and your thumb flips.

Where the formula comes from

The formula (a.y·b.z − a.z·b.y, …) didn't fall from the sky. It's derived by demanding that the operation has certain geometric properties and then asking: "what arithmetic gives me exactly that?" The derivation has three steps.

Step 1 — Write down what we want the operation to do

We start by listing the properties we need:

  1. Perpendicular. a × b is perpendicular to both a and b.
  2. Magnitude = parallelogram area. |a × b| = |a| |b| sin(θ) where θ is the angle between them.
  3. Right-handed orientation. If a is the world's x-axis and b is the y-axis, then a × b is the +z direction (not −z).
  4. Bilinear. The operation distributes over addition and scalar multiplication in each input: 
    (αu + βv) × w = α(u × w) + β(v × w)
u × (αv + βw) = α(u × v) + β(u × w)
    This property is what every "linear-feeling" operation has, including the dot product, matrix-vector multiplication, and so on. Without bilinearity, we couldn't expand parenthesized expressions in the way we expect.

That's the whole specification. Now we work out what arithmetic satisfies it.

Step 2 — Apply the properties to the basis vectors

Let x̂ = (1,0,0), ŷ = (0,1,0), ẑ = (0,0,1). We can immediately work out what cross-products of these basis vectors must be.

Same with same: x̂ × x̂ must be perpendicular to x̂ (so x-component is 0), and have magnitude |x̂||x̂|sin(0°) = 0. The only vector with zero magnitude is the zero vector. So x̂ × x̂ = (0,0,0). Same logic for ŷ × ŷ and ẑ × ẑ.

Different basis vectors: x̂ × ŷ must be perpendicular to both x̂ and ŷ, which leaves only the z-axis (up or down). Its magnitude is |x̂||ŷ|sin(90°) = 1·1·1 = 1. Right-handed orientation picks +z. So x̂ × ŷ = ẑ. By the same logic and cyclic symmetry:

x̂ × ŷ =  ẑ        ŷ × x̂ = -ẑ
ŷ × ẑ =  x̂        ẑ × ŷ = -x̂
ẑ × x̂ =  ŷ        x̂ × ẑ = -ŷ

The right-hand pairs come from property 4 applied with α = -1 (swapping inputs negates the result, which we can derive: a × a = 0 for any a, so (a + b) × (a + b) = 0; expanding by bilinearity gives a × b + b × a = 0, i.e. b × a = -(a × b)).

These nine basis-pair results are all the geometric information we need. Everything else follows from bilinearity.

Step 3 — Expand a general cross product using bilinearity

Any 3D vector can be written as a combination of basis vectors:

a = a.x·x̂ + a.y·ŷ + a.z·ẑ
b = b.x·x̂ + b.y·ŷ + b.z·ẑ

So:

a × b = (a.x·x̂ + a.y·ŷ + a.z·ẑ) × (b.x·x̂ + b.y·ŷ + b.z·ẑ)

By bilinearity, we expand this into all 9 pairwise products:

= a.x·b.x (x̂ × x̂)  +  a.x·b.y (x̂ × ŷ)  +  a.x·b.z (x̂ × ẑ)
+ a.y·b.x (ŷ × x̂)  +  a.y·b.y (ŷ × ŷ)  +  a.y·b.z (ŷ × ẑ)
+ a.z·b.x (ẑ × x̂)  +  a.z·b.y (ẑ × ŷ)  +  a.z·b.z (ẑ × ẑ)

Now substitute what each basis-pair cross product equals (from Step 2):

= a.x·b.x ·  0   +  a.x·b.y ·  ẑ   +  a.x·b.z · -ŷ
+ a.y·b.x · -ẑ   +  a.y·b.y ·  0   +  a.y·b.z ·  x̂
+ a.z·b.x ·  ŷ   +  a.z·b.y · -x̂   +  a.z·b.z ·  0

The three "same with same" terms vanish. The remaining six group naturally by which basis vector they contribute to:

x̂ coefficient:  a.y·b.z - a.z·b.y
ŷ coefficient:  a.z·b.x - a.x·b.z
ẑ coefficient:  a.x·b.y - a.y·b.x

And that's the formula. It's not a magical recipe — it's the unique arithmetic that satisfies the four geometric requirements we started from.

Why the cyclic pattern shows up

The cyclic order x → y → z → x in the formula is a direct reflection of the cyclic order of x̂ × ŷ = ẑ, ŷ × ẑ = x̂, ẑ × x̂ = ŷ. Those three identities are the definition of a right-handed coordinate system. The formula's pattern is just bookkeeping that propagates that orientation through the bilinear expansion.

This is also why the cross product is intrinsically 3D: the existence of a unique perpendicular direction (up to sign) only works in 3D. In 2D, the "perpendicular" of two vectors would just be a number (signed area). In 4D and up, there are too many perpendicular directions to pick from. 3D is special.

Where this lives in your codebase

Building a camera's right axis (lookAt)

Given the camera's forward direction f and a world "up" hint up (usually (0, 1, 0)), how do you find the camera's right axis?

const s = normalize(cross(f, up));

The cross product of "forward" and "up" produces a vector perpendicular to both — which is "right" by construction. Normalizing makes it unit length so we have a proper basis vector.

This is a perfect example of the cross product being the right tool: we don't care about the angle between forward and up; we just want a vector orthogonal to both, and we want a specific orientation (right, not left). The cross product gives us both with one operation.

Re-orthogonalizing the up axis (lookAt, continued)

const u = cross(s, f);

The world-up hint might not be exactly perpendicular to the camera's forward. The cross product of "right" and "forward" gives us a guaranteed-perpendicular "up" that's consistent with the camera's orientation. This is how lookAt produces a clean orthonormal basis even when the input "up" was approximate.

Surface normals from two edge vectors

If a triangle has edge vectors e1 and e2 along two of its sides, then cross(e1, e2) is perpendicular to the triangle's surface. Normalize and you have the surface normal for lighting. (Not used directly in your codebase yet — your lighting works on per-billboard normals derived from screen-space distance — but it's the standard technique once you start handling real meshes.)

Sign and orientation: why graphics is fussy about this

Because cross(a, b) = -cross(b, a), the order of arguments matters for orientation. Get the order wrong in lookAt and your camera ends up flipped (everything appears mirrored). Get the order wrong when computing a surface normal and your lighting goes inside-out (front faces look dark, back faces look bright).

The convention is: if you want a right-handed coordinate system (the standard in OpenGL/WebGL), the right axis is cross(forward, up) — not cross(up, forward). Memorize the order, or always verify with the right-hand rule on a known case.

Quick sanity check you can do in your head

Consider the basis vectors:

x̂ × ŷ = ẑ
ŷ × ẑ = x̂
ẑ × x̂ = ŷ

These three identities define the right-handed coordinate system. Plug them into the formula and verify — e.g. x̂ × ŷ = (1,0,0) × (0,1,0). Working through the formula:

x component: 0 * 0 - 0 * 1 = 0
y component: 0 * 0 - 1 * 0 = 0
z component: 1 * 1 - 0 * 0 = 1

Result: (0, 0, 1) = ẑ. ✓

If you ever forget the cyclic order, just reconstruct x̂ × ŷ = ẑ in your head — that tells you which component uses which inputs, and the rest follows by cyclic permutation.
